01

The Orb

Obsession: lag. The first small thing is a small sun.

Curve
v(t) = vfield · (1 − e−t/τ)
τ = m / k
0.40 s predicted, vortex particles
τ, measured
— press Yank to measure it on the GPU
Settles (95%)
1.19 s
Overshoot
0% drag is first-order; nothing bounces unless the field does
Step
1/120 s fixed, semi-implicit Euler, drag solved implicitly

Nothing in the orb is animated. Every particle is pushed by a field and held back by drag, so when you yank the spin it lags, catches up and settles. The curve isn’t chosen: it falls out of each particle’s mass-to-drag ratio, which is why the heavy dust trails the core by a second.

That lag is the whole effect. An orb that responds instantly reads as a video. One that has to catch up reads as stuff.

The measured τ comes in under the prediction because of pressure coupling. Particles drifting across the vortex’s shear pick up spin from the air around them, so they catch up faster than drag alone allows. Turn it off on the Drag node in the lab and the two numbers agree.

The Toggle

Obsession: overshoot, and how little of it you need.

Curve
damped spring, m = 1
Damping ζ
ζ = −ln(OS) / √(π² + ln²OS)
Overshoot
Peak
Settles (±2%)
Measured
— toggle the big switch to measure it
SwiftUI
CSS
the same spring, sampled into linear()

A switch that stops dead feels cheap, and one that bounces feels like a toy. At 8% the knob of a real-size switch travels 1.6 pixels past its stop. That’s too little to see, but enough to feel that something with mass arrived.

It’s a spring, not a curve, so there is no duration to restart. Tap it mid-flight and the knob keeps its velocity and turns around. The CSS version in the row below stops dead and starts again.

03

The Sheet

Obsession: the frame after you let go.

Release speed
— least-squares slope of the last 80 ms; zero if the finger rested
Projection
y + v/1000 · r/(1 − r) ; your last flick coasted —
Snap picked
— nearest snap to the projection
Spring
Rubber band
(1 − 1/(x·0.55/d + 1))·d d = 30% of the screen
Hand-off
— finger speed → first painted frame

The worst moment in a sheet is the frame after you let go. If the animation starts from rest, your throw is thrown away and the sheet hesitates before it moves. Here the spring starts at exactly the finger’s speed, so letting go has no seam.

Where it lands is decided the same way. The sheet goes where the flick would have carried it, using the deceleration iOS scroll views use, so a short fast flick and a long slow drag can both mean “open”.
